The assumption

A smartphone, data and a screen you can use

Healthcare, emergency assistance and public information increasingly assume a smartphone, reliable internet, digital literacy and the physical ability to navigate an interface. Millions of people have none of the four.

Community healthcare

Paper, handwritten registers, a journey to ask

Many facilities still run on paper records and physical visits. Patients travel to a hospital just to book an appointment, ask a question or follow up on their care, and some facilities have no computer at all. Information is fragmented, patients struggle to reach care and health workers carry the administrative load.

Who is left furthest behind

A phone is not the same as access

People with disabilities, older people, people with limited literacy and patients whose condition makes screens hard are hit hardest. Having a telephone does not mean having the information, the people or the services you need at a critical moment.

{{ rule(n.v, "What Talk does") }}{% set n.v = n.v + 1 %}

1. Call

Say what you need

Ring one number from any phone and speak in plain English. "I need a clinic near Surulere for antenatal care." Nothing to install and no data to buy.

2. Choose

You decide, not Talk

Talk asks every connected provider at the same moment and tells you the real options: how far, how long the wait, what it costs. You pick. An area is not a hospital, so Talk never picks for you.

3. Booked and followed up

Your time, then a check-in

Talk books your time in the clinic's own appointment book and texts you the details. It reminds you before the visit and checks in after it. If you did not come, it reaches out to help you rebook.

Where a hospital has no computer system, the appointment book Talk keeps for it is the system. Everything Talk can do · How it works in detail

Live

Coordinates appointments and enquiries

Find the nearest clinic, hospital or pharmacy, see the wait and the fee, book and cancel, check whether a medicine is in stock.

Live

Gives approved information

A caller's basic question is answered only from the facility's own patient guide, never from the model's memory. A warning sign is always answered first, exactly as the facility wrote it.

Live

Manages follow-up

A reminder the day before, a thank-you to the patient who came, an offer to rebook to the one who did not, and the care team's replies texted back.

Live

Routes requests to the right people

A booking goes to the provider the caller chose. A question the guide does not cover goes to that facility's staff, on their own front desk.

Live

Escalates what needs a person

Emergency words get the emergency number at once, and the nearest emergency care is told on its front desk, urgent first, with a text to staff if a number is set.

Always

Clinical decisions stay with people

Talk gives general information and coordinates care. It does not diagnose. Healthcare workers and trained front-desk assistants stay at the centre of the model.

{{ rule(n.v, "Why we start with community healthcare") }}{% set n.v = n.v + 1 %}

Two community hospitals have given us letters of intent to deploy Talk. In those conversations we found they lack even basic computing to organise their work, and putting AI into a facility that cannot organise its own information would solve only part of the problem. So the deployment has three parts.

1. Digital infrastructure

Computers and connectivity

Giving a participating facility the essential computers and internet connection it does not have.

2. Information organisation

Structure what is on paper

Helping the facility structure its existing operational and patient-service information, so authorised staff and AI systems can work with it.

3. AI-enabled coordination

Talk

Appointment management, patient enquiries, follow-up, request routing and administrative workflows, supporting the staff rather than replacing them.

The build on this site runs against four simulated providers until those facilities are connected: the same contract a real one uses, with made-up places, queues and patients.

Talk answers in English, at any hour

{% if not s.hub_test_number %}We send you the number once you ask. {% endif %}Use a quiet place, hold the phone close, and say one short sentence at a time. Wait for Talk to finish before you answer.

What to say

  1. "Find the nearest clinic for antenatal care in Surulere." Talk lists the nearest places with their waiting time and fee, and offers to book.
  2. "Book an appointment in Yaba." Talk offers the clinics there. Say "the cheaper one", then "tomorrow morning", then "yes".
  3. "Do you have amoxicillin in stock near Ikeja?" Talk names the pharmacies that have it, and the price.
  4. "What should I bring to my first visit at the cancer centre?" Talk answers from the centre's own patient guide.
  5. "Cancel my appointment." Talk reads it back and cancels it with the clinic that holds it.

What happens next

  • You get a text with the clinic, the day and time, and your booking code.
  • The booking appears in that provider's own appointment book. Open its front desk page to see it.
  • The day before, Talk texts a reminder. After the visit it thanks you, or, if you did not come, helps you rebook.

Hearing and understanding

Muse Voice Transcribe hears the caller. Muse Spark understands what they mean.

Muse Spark reads each sentence by meaning, so "my baby needs her jabs" becomes a search for child immunisation near the caller. Anything it fills in must be made of the caller's own words, and every fact a caller hears comes from a provider, never from the model.
